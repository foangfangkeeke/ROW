Stochastic Spatiotemporal Right-of-Way Allocation
for Nighttime Autonomous Freight Transportation
1. Introduction
Freight transportation is essential to the functioning of modern supply chains, yet its
operation is increasingly constrained by limited road capacity, rising labor costs, driver
shortages, and stringent service requirements. These challenges are particularly pronounced
for overnight freight movements. Although nighttime operations can avoid daytime conges-
tion and make use of otherwise underutilized transportation capacity, they rely heavily on
drivers working irregular hours and operating under fatigue-prone conditions. Consequently,
nighttime freight transportation presents both an important operational opportunity and a
persistent labor and safety challenge.
Recent advances in autonomous driving, vehicle sensing, digital infrastructure, and fleet
coordination have created new possibilities for addressing these challenges. Compared with
daytime urban passenger transportation, nighttime freight operations may provide a partic-
ularly suitable early application for autonomous vehicles. Freight movements are generally
planned in advance, controlled by professional operators, and concentrated along relatively
stable origin–destination corridors. Nighttime traffic volumes are also typically lower, allow-
ing autonomous trucks to operate in a less congested and less behaviorally complex envi-
ronment. Autonomous freight operations can therefore potentially exploit unused nighttime
road capacity, reduce dependence on overnight driving labor, lower operating costs, and limit
workers’ exposure to fatigue-related risks. These characteristics make nighttime autonomous
logistics a potentially important transitional application between conventional trucking and
fully autonomous road transportation.
The emergence of autonomous trucks, however, does not imply that autonomous freight
services can be deployed efficiently on every road. Fully driverless vehicles may initially
operate only within restricted operational design domains, and their performance can dete-
riorate when they interact extensively with heterogeneous human-driven traffic. Moreover,
a shipment can be completed without a human driver only if autonomous operating condi-
tions are available over its entire route. An isolated autonomous segment is of limited value
when a vehicle still requires a driver to access that segment or to complete the remainder of
the trip. The deployment of autonomous freight therefore involves an importantend-to-end
connectivity requirement: autonomous road access must form a temporally and spatially
continuous path between the shipment’s origin and destination.
One possible way to provide such operating conditions is to allocate selected road capac-
ity temporarily to autonomous freight vehicles. In this paper, we use the termright-of-way
(ROW) to denote a completely isolated or strictly access-controlled road lane that is reserved
for eligible autonomous trucks during a specified time period. Unlike the construction of per-
manent dedicated infrastructure, nighttime ROW allocation can reuse existing road capacity\n\nand can be adjusted across locations and time periods. A lane may, for example, remain
available to general traffic during the evening peak, be designated as an autonomous freight
ROW during low-demand nighttime hours, and return to general use before the morning
peak.
This operational flexibility creates a new planning problem. Transportation authorities
or infrastructure operators must determine which road segments should be designated as
autonomous freight ROWs and during which nighttime periods. These decisions must be
made before the exact freight demand and available road capacity are known. Once demand
is realized, shipments can be assigned either to the resulting autonomous ROW network or to
conventional human-driven trucking routes. The former may reduce driver and energy costs
but requires a complete ROW path, whereas the latter can use the general road network but
remains subject to conventional transportation costs and congestion.
Three interdependencies make this planning problem nontrivial. First, ROW decisions
exhibit strong spatial and temporal complementarity. Opening one isolated link may gen-
erate little or no operational benefit, whereas opening a sequence of compatible link–period
combinations can create an end-to-end autonomous freight service. Second, the value of a
ROW configuration depends on uncertain freight demand and uncertain effective road ca-
pacity. A configuration designed for expected demand may perform poorly when the realized
spatial distribution of freight differs from its forecast. Third, ROW allocation involves an
opportunity cost when a reserved lane is converted from capacity previously available to gen-
eral traffic. Expanding the autonomous freight network may reduce autonomous operating
costs while simultaneously limiting the capacity available to conventional trucks.
These observations motivate the following research questions:
1. Where and when should temporary autonomous freight ROWs be activated under
limited deployment resources?
2. How should uncertain freight demand be allocated between end-to-end autonomous
transportation and conventional trucking after uncertainty is realized?
3. How do demand uncertainty, background traffic, ROW resource availability, and the
cost advantages of autonomous transportation affect the structure and value of the
resulting ROW network?
To address these questions, we formulate a two-stage stochastic network design model.
In the first stage, a planner selects the road-segment and time-period combinations to be
operated as autonomous freight ROWs, subject to an aggregate ROW deployment resource
constraint. In the second stage, after freight demand and effective road capacities are real-
ized, a centralized dispatcher determines the routing and mode of each divisible freight flow.
Freight assigned to autonomous transportation must follow a complete origin-to-destination
path composed exclusively of activated ROW arcs. The remaining freight is explicitly routed
through the general road network using conventional trucks. An emergency outsourcing or
postponement option guarantees feasibility in extreme scenarios and represents the high cost
of failing to serve freight through the planned network.
The model is constructed on a two-layer time-expanded network. The autonomous layer
represents movements that are feasible only when the corresponding ROW remains active
2\n\nthroughout each link traversal, whereas the conventional layer represents movements through
the general road network. This representation simultaneously captures vehicle movement
over space, origin-dispatch timing, delivery deadlines, and end-to-end autonomous-route
continuity. Freight in either layer may wait at its origin logistics terminal before dispatch,
but neither autonomous nor conventional trucks may wait at intermediate road nodes. The
construction also avoids explicitly enumerating the potentially enormous set of space–time
paths.
Our work is related to three streams of literature. The first studies deterministic and
stochastic network design with multicommodity flows (????). The second considers trans-
portation network design with end-to-end service requirements (??). The third investigates
dedicated infrastructure and operating strategies for autonomous vehicles and autonomous
truck fleets (??). Despite their relevance, these streams do not jointly address the temporary
allocation of road space across both locations and time periods, uncertain freight demand and
effective capacity, end-to-end autonomous-route requirements, and explicit recourse routing
through both autonomous and conventional transportation networks. The present study
connects these elements in a unified stochastic optimization framework.
This paper makes four main contributions. First, we introduce a stochastic spatiotem-
poral ROW design problem for nighttime autonomous freight transportation. In contrast
to permanent lane construction models, the proposed framework treats road access as a
time-dependent and reversible operational resource. It also explicitly captures the require-
ment that driverless freight must have a complete ROW connection from its origin to its
destination.
Second, we develop an integrated two-stage stochastic optimization model that jointly
considers infrastructure access decisions, freight-mode assignment, and conventional and
autonomous freight routing. The model captures both the operating-cost advantage of au-
tonomous transportation and, when ROWs are converted from existing lanes, the associated
capacity opportunity cost imposed on conventional freight. Because aggregate freight flows
are modeled as continuous, the second-stage problem is a linear multicommodity flow prob-
lem that can be solved exactly and decomposed by demand scenario.
Third, we establish structural properties of the recourse problem and exploit them in an
exact decomposition algorithm. In particular, the second-stage value function is finite, con-
vex, continuous, and piecewise linear over the relaxed first-stage domain. These properties
enable a parallel multi-cut Benders decomposition method. We further reduce computa-
tional burden through shortest-time window screening, exact forward–backward reachabil-
ity, budget-aware autonomous-path pruning, and unused ROW-candidate elimination. The
resulting procedures exploit both temporal feasibility and the first-stage deployment budget
while preserving global optimality.
Fourth, the model provides a framework for identifying when nighttime autonomous
ROW deployment creates meaningful system value. The computational study is designed
to examine how optimal ROW configurations change with the magnitude and spatial con-
centration of freight demand, the degree of demand and capacity uncertainty, background
nighttime traffic, autonomous operating-cost savings, conventional-road capacity losses, and
the available ROW deployment budget. It also quantifies the value of stochastic planning and
identifies conditions under which locally attractive ROW segments fail to generate system-
level benefits because they do not form an end-to-end autonomous corridor.

2. Literature Review
This study lies at the intersection of autonomous-freight infrastructure planning, stochas-
tic service network design, and network design with end-to-end service requirements. We
review these three streams of literature and then position the present study with respect to
their modeling and algorithmic foundations.
2.1. Autonomous Freight Transportation and Dedicated Road Infrastructure
Autonomous driving technologies have attracted growing interest in freight transporta-
tion because long-haul trucking is characterized by high labor costs, driver shortages, hours-
of-service restrictions, and relatively repetitive operating environments. Cost analyses sug-
gest that removing or reducing the dependence on onboard drivers can alter both the operat-
ingcostandutilizationpatternofheavytrucks(?). Unlikehuman-driventrucks, autonomous
trucks may also operate for longer periods without being directly constrained by driver rest
requirements. These potential advantages are particularly relevant to nighttime operations,
during which road capacity is often less intensively used but conventional carriers must rely
on drivers working undesirable and fatigue-prone shifts.
The realization of these benefits, however, depends on where autonomous trucks are per-
mitted and technically capable of operating. During the transition toward full automation,
autonomous vehicles are likely to coexist with human-driven vehicles and may be restricted
to specific operational design domains. This has motivated research on autonomous-vehicle
zones, dedicated lanes, and other forms of segregated infrastructure.
A seminal network-level study is?, who investigate the optimal design of autonomous-
vehicle zones in a transportation network. Their model captures the interaction between
infrastructure deployment and routing behavior through a mixed-routing equilibrium. This
workdemonstratesthatautonomous-vehicleinfrastructureshouldbe designedatthenetwork
level because the performance of one designated segment depends on how it connects to
the remainder of the network. Related studies of connected and autonomous vehicle lane
management examine how dedicated lanes affect road capacity, traffic performance, safety,
and the distribution of autonomous and human-driven vehicles across lanes.
Most of this literature is motivated by passenger transportation and focuses on traffic
equilibrium, autonomous-vehicle penetration, or the capacity effects of lane segregation.
Infrastructure decisions are generally modeled as static or long-term decisions. As a result,
the temporal flexibility of road space—for example, retaining a lane for general traffic during
peak periods and reserving it for autonomous freight during selected nighttime periods—has
received limited attention.
More recently,?study dedicated-lane planning for autonomous truck fleets under hours-
of-service regulations. Their work is particularly relevant because it links autonomous-truck
infrastructure with freight operations and explicitly recognizes the labor-related value of
4\n\nautonomous trucking. Nevertheless, their focus differs from ours in several respects. In
particular, the present study treats ROW allocation as a temporary link–period decision, in-
corporatesuncertain freightdemandand effective road capacity, andallows realized freightto
be routed through either an end-to-end autonomous network or a conventional road network.
The distinction between a dedicated lane and the ROW considered in this paper is im-
portant. We do not assume that a permanent autonomous-truck lane must be constructed.
Instead, an existing lane or controlled road facility can be temporarily designated as a com-
pletelyisolatedorstrictlyaccess-controlledautonomousfreightROW.Theresultingplanning
problem is therefore not only where autonomous infrastructure should be provided, but also
when each candidate road segment should be made available to autonomous freight.

and how multicommodity flows should be routed through the activated network (?). These

supply chain planning.
Because freight demand is rarely known with certainty at the time that network decisions
are made, stochastic service network design has received substantial attention.?investigate

the quality of a network configuration depends not only on its performance under average

Subsequent studies have developed more general formulations and scalable algorithms for
stochastic network design.?consider a two-stage stochastic multicommodity network design

Our model shares the two-stage design-and-flow structure of this literature, but the in-
terpretation and coupling of the decisions are different. The first-stage variables do not
construct permanent arcs or schedule conventional transportation services. They allocate a
limited road-use resource across physical links and time periods. A ROW decision simul-
taneously creates capacity in the autonomous layer and, when the ROW is converted from
an existing lane, may reduce capacity in the conventional layer. Thus, the same first-stage
decision can produce both a capacity benefit and a capacity opportunity cost.
The second-stage structure also differs from conventional service network design. After
demand and effective capacity are realized, each freight flow can be served using one of two
operationally distinct networks. Autonomous freight can use only activated ROW arcs and
must have a continuous origin-to-destination ROW path. Freight that cannot be served

general road network. The recourse problem therefore integrates mode assignment and
routing instead of treating nonautonomous freight as an exogenous residual demand.

optimal solution.
2.3. Network Design with End-to-End Service Requirements
The third literature stream concerns network design with end-to-end service require-
ments. Standard network design formulations typically enforce flow conservation and arc
capacities but do not necessarily guarantee that every selected path satisfies a path-level
service condition. In many applications, however, service feasibility depends on cumulative
or path-specific characteristics such as total transit time, latency, reliability, or the number
of transfers.
?formulateamulticommoditynetworkdesignprobleminwhicheachorigin-to-destination
route must satisfy end-to-end service requirements. They develop strong valid inequalities
and polyhedral methods for the resulting formulation.?subsequently develop a path-based
formulation and a branch-and-cut-and-price framework to solve larger instances with path-
level service restrictions.
The end-to-end requirement in the present study has a different operational meaning. It
does not simply impose a maximum travel time or another quality-of-service threshold on a
freight path. Instead, it determines whether the shipment can be operated without a human
driver. A freight movement is classified as autonomous only if every physical link along its
origin-to-destination trajectory is covered by an active ROW throughout the correspond-
ing traversal. Opening an isolated ROW segment may therefore produce no autonomous
transportation benefit if the remaining segments of the trip require conventional driving.
This feature creates strong complementarity among ROW decisions. Consider a shipment
whose only feasible autonomous route consists of two consecutive road segments. Activating
either segment alone does not create an autonomous route, whereas activating both segments
allows the entire shipment to be transported autonomously. Therefore, the value of a ROW
segment cannot generally be evaluated independently, and the benefit generated by a set of
ROW decisions is not generally submodular. This distinguishes the problem from network
design settings in which individual capacity additions yield separable or diminishing returns.
The explicit conventional-trucking alternative is also important. A shipment that lacks
an end-to-end ROW path is not necessarily rejected. It can be reassigned to a human-driven
truck and routed through the general road network. The planner must therefore evaluate
whether the savings obtained by creating an autonomous corridor justify both the required
ROW resources and any conventional-road capacity that is displaced.
6\n\n2.4. Decomposition Methods and Positioning of This Study
Benders decomposition is widely used for capacitated and stochastic network design be-
cause fixing the binary design decisions typically leaves a linear multicommodity flow prob-
lem. Classical acceleration strategies include generating Pareto-optimal cuts (?), retaining
selected flow information in the master problem (?), and adaptively aggregating and disag-
gregating scenarios (?). Large stochastic problems can also benefit from scenario sampling,
parallel subproblem solution, and dual averaging (?).
Our continuous-flow formulation preserves this favorable decomposition structure. Con-
ditional on a first-stage ROW schedule, each scenario produces a linear two-layer multicom-
modity flow problem. Its dual variables quantify the marginal value of autonomous ROW
capacity and the marginal cost of capacity removed from the conventional road network.
These dual quantities lead directly to scenario-specific Benders optimality cuts.
The algorithmic contribution of this paper does not lie in proposing a completely new
decomposition paradigm. Instead, we exploit the particular structure of the nighttime ROW
problem through a tailored exact implementation. This implementation combines paral-
lel multi-cut Benders decomposition with shortest-time window screening, exact forward–
backward reachability, budget-aware autonomous-path pruning, and unused ROW-candidate
elimination. These procedures reduce both the master problem and the scenario subproblems
while preserving global optimality.
In summary, the existing autonomous-vehicle infrastructure literature explains the traffic
and routing implications of segregated facilities but primarily considers static designs and
passenger traffic. The stochastic service network design literature provides a rigorous frame-
work for design decisions under uncertain demand and capacity but does not distinguish
between end-to-end autonomous transportation and conventional freight recourse. The end-
to-end network design literature captures path-level service feasibility but does not consider
temporary road access, stochastic freight recourse, or capacity transfers between autonomous
and conventional transportation.
The present study connects these streams by jointly considering (i) temporary link–
period ROW allocation, (ii) uncertain freight demand and effective road capacity, (iii) end-
to-end eligibility for driverless transportation, (iv) explicit routing of both autonomous and
conventional freight, and (v) the opportunity cost of reallocating road capacity. To the best
of our knowledge, these features have not been integrated within a single stochastic network
design framework.
3. Problem Description
3.1. Operating setting
Consider a road authority that can reserve selected road capacity for autonomous freight
vehicles during the night. We use ROW to mean a completely separated or strictly access-
controlled lane that can be used by autonomous trucks but not by passenger cars or conven-
tional trucks while the designation is in effect. The designation is temporary. A lane may
remain in general use during the evening peak, operate as an autonomous-freight ROW for
several nighttime periods, and return to general use before the morning peak.
The authority does not know the exact freight demand or effective road capacity when
it announces the ROW schedule. Demand varies from night to night, and capacity can be

This decision sequence gives rise to two distinct planning stages. In the first stage, the
authority chooses the link–period combinations that will operate as ROWs, subject to a
deployment resource limit. In the second stage, freight demand and usable capacities are
observed, after which the dispatcher determines the autonomous and conventional freight
flows. Figure 1 summarizes the sequence.
Two features distinguish this setting from a standard lane-opening problem. First, au-
tonomous service is an end-to-end product. Opening a short isolated segment may save travel
time on that segment, but it does not remove the driver if the same shipment still requires
conventional driving before or after the segment. Second, ROW allocation may transfer road
capacity between two freight technologies. An activated ROW creates protected capacity for
autonomous trucks. If the ROW is converted from an existing general-purpose lane, it also
removes capacity from conventional traffic. Hence, opening more ROWs is not automatically
better.
The planner minimizes the sum of ROW deployment cost and expected freight operating
cost. Autonomous operating costs incorporate the savings associated with reduced onboard
labor and, when appropriate, more stable driving conditions and lower energy use. We do
not model vehicle platoons explicitly. Their average operational effect can be represented
through autonomous arc costs and capacities approximately without introducing truck-level
synchronization variables.
3.2. Modeling assumptions
The formulation is developed under the following assumptions.
Assumption 1.Freight movements are centrally coordinated and represented as divisible
aggregate flows.
Assumption 2.A shipment may be held at its origin logistics terminal before dispatch.
After dispatch, it travels continuously to its destination without waiting at intermediate road
nodes. An autonomous shipment must remain in the autonomous layer and use active ROWs
throughout every link traversal.
8\n\nAssumption 3.Nighttime passenger traffic is exogenous, and its effect on freight transporta-
tion is represented by the scenario-dependent residual capacities of general-purpose roads.
Assumption 4.Freight that cannot be accommodated by either transportation mode may be
assigned to an emergency service at a sufficiently high unit cost.
Assumption 1 is consistent with the tactical nature of the planning problem. Nighttime
freight operations are typically coordinated by a logistics operator or a digital freight plat-
form, and the demand associated with an origin–destination pair represents a collection of
shipments rather than an individual vehicle. Freight quantities can therefore be measured
in truckloads or equivalent continuous units. Assumption 2 distinguishes terminal holding
from en-route waiting. Both autonomous and conventional freight may choose a later dis-
patch time at the origin logistics terminal, but a dispatched truck cannot stop at an ordinary
road intersection for one or more model periods. Signal delay and ordinary traffic delay are
incorporated in the layer-specific link travel times rather than represented by discretionary
waiting arcs. The same assumption also captures the end-to-end nature of autonomous
freight service. A shipment is regarded as autonomous only when every road segment used
along its trajectory is covered by an active ROW throughout the time required to traverse
that segment. An isolated ROW segment does not eliminate the need for a human driver
if conventional driving is still required elsewhere along the trip. Consequently, a shipment
without a temporally continuous ROW path must be transported conventionally or assigned
to the emergency option. Assumption 3 reflects the focus on nighttime operations, during
which passenger volumes are generally lower than those observed in daytime peak periods.
Ratherthanmodelingpassengerroutechoicesendogenously, theireffects, togetherwithother
sources of capacity variation, are incorporated into the scenario-dependent residual road ca-
pacities. This treatment preserves the freight-planning focus of the model while allowing the
consequences of uncertain background traffic to be examined. Finally, Assumption 4 guar-
antees relatively complete recourse for every first-stage ROW schedule and every realized
scenario. The emergency cost is set above the cost of regular autonomous and conventional
transportation so that this option is used only when the planned road system cannot accom-
modate all realized demand. Its use therefore provides an explicit measure of freight service
failure rather than merely serving as a mathematical feasibility device.

5. Solution Approach
5.1. Decomposition structure
The model has a useful decomposition structure. The first stage contains only the bi-
nary ROW decisionsy, whereas fixingyleaves one continuous linear recourse problem for
each scenario. Moreover, the scenario problems are mutually independent. The emergency
transportation option makes the recourse problem feasible for everyy∈Yand everyω∈Ω.
Hence, the model has relatively complete recourse and only Benders optimality cuts are
required.
We exploit this structure through a parallel multi-cut Benders method. Each iteration
solves a master problem in the ROW variables and then evaluates the resulting schedule
in all scenarios. One optimality cut is generated for every scenario whose recourse cost is
underestimated. Two features are retained to address the network structure of the problem.
First, Pareto-oriented dual solutions are used to reduce the effect of dual degeneracy in
15\n\nthe time-space flow problems. Second, a small number of autonomous cut-set inequalities
are added to the recourse formulations to expose the end-to-end ROW requirement more
directly. Before optimization, four exact reduction rules screen time-infeasible nodes, remove
unreachable states, eliminate autonomous arcs that cannot appear under the ROW budget,
and delete unused master variables.

In implementation, the dual multipliers are read under a single standardized row conven-
tion, and every candidate cut is checked for tightness at its generation point. The equality
defining the optimal dual face is enforced at the solver feasibility tolerance. If the auxiliary
problem is numerically infeasible or its cut fails the tightness check, the ordinary optimal dual
from the recourse solve is used. This fallback preserves exactness because it still generates a
valid optimality cut at the underestimated schedule.
Scenario-level parallelism is used before any lower-level parallelization. To make timing
comparisons reproducible, the master receives one thread and the remaining thread budget
is divided among scenario workers. All generated exact cuts are retained. No dynamic
relaxation cuts, resource-cover separation, or cut-pool deletion rules are used in the main
implementation.
The standard finite-convergence argument for integer Benders decomposition applies.
The cut-set inequalities do not alter the recourse function, and each accepted Pareto-oriented
cut or fallback ordinary cut is an exact optimality cut that is tight at the evaluated schedule.
If a schedule is underestimated, at least one violated cut is added. Because the first-stage
set contains finitely many binary ROW schedules, Algorithm 1 terminates finitely with an
optimal solution when the scenario set is finite, all optimization problems are solved exactly,
andε= 0.
